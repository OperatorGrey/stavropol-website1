The history: from the fortress of the north Caucasus to the administrative center.

Stavropol in early days

According to legend, the city owes its name to a stone cross of unknown origin that was discovered by the Hopersky Cossacks—the builders of the first fortress. Since then, the area has been known as Stavropol (which translates from Greek as “stavros” meaning “cross” and “polis” meaning “city”). All the routes leading to the Volga and the Don came under its “supervision.” In addition, the fortress and its surroundings were used as a venue for diplomatic negotiations.

Stavropol as a settlement

All the routes leading to the Volga and the Don came under its “supervision.” In addition, the fortress and its surroundings were used as a venue for diplomatic negotiations. Over time, small settlements in the form of soldiers’ settlements and Cossack villages sprang up around the military observation post. Just six years after the fortress was built, Stavropol’s rapid development transformed it into an economic and administrative center. In the early 19th century, the city’s territory was actively settled by both immigrants from Russian provinces (Kursk, Tambov, and Voronezh) and foreigners: for example, in 1809, the authorities invited 50 Armenian families to settle there.

Stavropol during the monarchy

In 1845, the first drama theater in the Caucasus began attracting audiences. Book lovers in Stavropol founded the first public pay-to-use library in 1852, which held about 600 books.

Stavropol in early 20th century

In 1935, the city was renamed in honor of the military commander Kliment Voroshilov and became known as Voroshilovsk. The German occupiers held on to the captured city of Voroshilovsk for just over five months (from August 3, 1942, to January 21, 1943). In 1943, the city was renamed back to Stavropol.

Stavropol in late 20th century

During the cold war Stavropol was a strong, communist agriculture-centred region. Stavropol was the small motherland for the last General Secretary, First and the last president of the USSR, Michail Gorbochev. After the USSR collaps in 1991, the region still supported the communistic party (Communist Party of the Russian Federation) and stayed in a, so called, ‘Red Belt’, a group of regions that supported communists from 1991 to 1999-2000

Stavropol in our days

By current days, Stavropol did not change his principals and still is a small, quite town in the middle of nowhere.
